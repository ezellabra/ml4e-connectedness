\section{Introduction}
% Budget: 1 page.

This paper asks how measured connectedness among emerging equity markets changes with the method used to estimate it, and what share of it reflects a global common factor rather than idiosyncratic links between markets. We study weekly returns of US-listed country funds for 17 emerging markets in Latin America, Asia and EMEA, together with the S\&P~500 as a global reference, from March 2011 to October 2026, and we estimate connectedness in four ways: the variance-decomposition measures of \citet{diebold2014network} on a VAR estimated by OLS; the same measures on a sparse VAR estimated by the lasso with time-series cross-validation; NETS \citep{barigozzi2019nets}, which estimates sparse Granger, contemporaneous and long-run partial-correlation networks; and FNETS \citep{barigozzi2024fnets}, which estimates the same networks after removing the common factor. All methods are estimated on the same rolling windows and mapped into comparable measures, and we compare them on three criteria: the density of the networks, the agreement between methods, and their stability over time.

We test three hypotheses. First, regularization alone should matter little for total connectedness at the weekly frequency, because the cross-market dependence of returns is mostly contemporaneous rather than lagged. Second, a substantial share of the connectedness measured by Diebold--Y{\i}lmaz should be due to a common factor, so that it falls markedly once the factor is removed, and its time variation should follow the weight of that factor. Third, sparse estimators should produce networks that are less dense, and also less stable from one window to the next, than the dense variance-decomposition network.

The results support the first two hypotheses and only partly the third. The lasso VAR sets almost all lag coefficients to zero yet gives nearly the same connectedness as Diebold--Y{\i}lmaz (80.0\% against 81.0\% on average over windows, correlation 0.98). NETS gives a lower level (61.5\%) and keeps about half of the possible long-run links, while tracking the Diebold--Y{\i}lmaz series closely (correlation 0.89). FNETS finds a single common factor in every window, which explains on average 58\% of the variance of the returns; Measured on the same scale as Diebold--Y{\i}lmaz, spillovers between the idiosyncratic components account on average for only 19.6\% of the variance of a market, against the 81.0\% that Diebold--Y{\i}lmaz attributes to shocks in other markets, and a benchmark with the same common factor and no links between markets already produces 81.1\% connectedness. Equivalently, total connectedness falls by 41\% when the factor is removed, a share that is stable across window lengths, horizons, the exclusion of the S\&P~500 and the exclusion of 2020, although it changes when two factors are imposed. Over time, Diebold--Y{\i}lmaz connectedness and the weight of the common factor move together. The sparse networks are indeed much less dense, but stability depends on the measure: NETS connectedness jumps more from one window to the next than Diebold--Y{\i}lmaz, yet its strongest long-run links are more persistent; the idiosyncratic network of FNETS is both the sparsest and the least stable. South Africa, Korea and Taiwan are the main net transmitters and Turkey, Vietnam and Argentina the main net receivers in the dense and NETS systems; once the common factor is removed, Argentina is close to neutral.

Our contribution is to put the base paper's connectedness measures side by side with sparse and factor-adjusted network estimators on the same data, windows and metrics, for a set of emerging markets, and to quantify the part of measured connectedness that is a common factor. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data, Section~\ref{sec:methods} the methods and comparison criteria, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, and Sections~6 and~7 discuss them and conclude. Robustness checks, country-level results and bootstrap intervals are in the appendix.
